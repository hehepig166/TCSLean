# The Lovász Local Lemma

These notes present the classical Lovász Local Lemma (LLL) in a self-contained form. The main theme is the following: a family of undesirable events may be simultaneously avoidable even when the union bound is useless, provided that each undesirable event is sufficiently unlikely and depends on only a limited number of the others.

The discussion includes the dependency-graph formalism, the asymmetric and symmetric forms of the LLL, a complete proof of the asymmetric form, and two applications: bounded-dependency $k$-SAT and coloring uniform hypergraphs.

---

## 1. Probability-theoretic preliminaries

Let $(\Omega,\mathcal F,\Pr)$ be a probability space, where:

- $\Omega$ is the sample space;
- $\mathcal F$ is a $\sigma$-algebra of events;
- $\Pr:\mathcal F\to[0,1]$ is a probability measure.

For an event $A\in\mathcal F$, its complement is denoted by

$$
\overline A \coloneqq \Omega\setminus A.
$$

For a positive integer $r$, write

$$
[r]\coloneqq \{1,2,\ldots,r\}.
$$

An intersection over an empty index set is defined to be $\Omega$, and a product over an empty index set is defined to be $1$.

### 1.1 Independence of events

Two events $A,B\in\mathcal F$ are **independent** if

$$
\Pr(A\cap B)=\Pr(A)\Pr(B).
$$

Equivalently, if $\Pr(B)>0$, then

$$
\Pr(A\mid B)=\Pr(A).
$$

The following stronger notion is the one needed for dependency graphs.

### Definition 1.1 — Independence from a family of events

Let $A,A_1,\ldots,A_t\in\mathcal F$. We say that $A$ is **independent of the family** $\{A_1,\ldots,A_t\}$ if $A$ is independent of every event of the form

$$
B=B_1\cap B_2\cap\cdots\cap B_t,
$$

where, for every $s\in[t]$, either $B_s=A_s$ or $B_s=\overline{A_s}$.

Equivalently, whenever $\Pr(B)>0$,

$$
\Pr(A\mid B)=\Pr(A).
$$

This definition says that conditioning on any pattern of occurrence/non-occurrence among $A_1,\ldots,A_t$ does not change the probability of $A$.

---

## 2. The probabilistic method and the limitation of the union bound

Suppose that $A_1,\ldots,A_m\in\mathcal F$ are **bad events**. We want to prove that there exists an outcome $\omega\in\Omega$ for which none of the bad events occurs. Equivalently, we want to show

$$
\Pr\!\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^m \overline{A_i}\right)>0.
$$

The union bound gives

$$
\Pr\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^m A_i\right)
\le \sum_{i=1}^m \Pr(A_i).
$$

Hence, if

$$
\sum_{i=1}^m \Pr(A_i)<1,
$$

then

$$
\Pr\!\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^m \overline{A_i}\right)
=1-\Pr\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^m A_i\right)>0.
$$

In particular, if $\Pr(A_i)\le p$ for every $i$, then the union bound succeeds whenever

$$
mp<1.
$$

This criterion depends on the **total number** $m$ of bad events. The Lovász Local Lemma replaces this global requirement by a condition involving only the **local dependency structure** among the bad events.

---

## 3. Dependency graphs

Let $A_1,\ldots,A_m\in\mathcal F$ be events.

### Definition 3.1 — Dependency graph

A finite simple undirected graph

$$
G=([m],E)
$$

is called a **dependency graph** for $A_1,\ldots,A_m$ if, for every $i\in[m]$, the event $A_i$ is independent of the family

$$
\{A_j: j\in[m]\setminus(\Gamma(i)\cup\{i\})\},
$$

where

$$
\Gamma(i)\coloneqq \{j\in[m]\setminus\{i\}: \{i,j\}\in E\}
$$

is the neighborhood of vertex $i$ in $G$.

Thus $A_i$ may depend on the events indexed by $\Gamma(i)$, but it must be independent, in the strong sense of Definition 1.1, of all non-neighboring events.

The **dependency degree** is the maximum degree of the dependency graph:

$$
d\coloneqq \max_{i\in[m]} |\Gamma(i)|.
$$

### 3.1 Dependency graphs generated by independent random variables

A common source of dependency graphs is the following variable model.

Let $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ be mutually independent random variables. For each bad event $A_i$, let

$$
\operatorname{vbl}(A_i)\subseteq[n]
$$

be a set of variable indices such that whether $A_i$ occurs is completely determined by the random variables

$$
\{X_r:r\in\operatorname{vbl}(A_i)\}.
$$

Define a graph on $[m]$ by joining distinct vertices $i$ and $j$ exactly when

$$
\operatorname{vbl}(A_i)\cap\operatorname{vbl}(A_j)\neq\varnothing.
$$

This graph is a valid dependency graph.

### Proposition 3.2

The variable-overlap graph defined above is a dependency graph for $A_1,\ldots,A_m$.

#### Proof

Fix $i\in[m]$, and let $S\subseteq[m]\setminus(\Gamma(i)\cup\{i\})$. For every $j\in S$,

$$
\operatorname{vbl}(A_i)\cap\operatorname{vbl}(A_j)=\varnothing.
$$

Therefore

$$
\operatorname{vbl}(A_i)
\cap
\bigcup_{j\in S}\operatorname{vbl}(A_j)
=\varnothing.
$$

The event $A_i$ is determined only by the variables indexed by $\operatorname{vbl}(A_i)$, whereas every Boolean combination of the events $\{A_j:j\in S\}$ is determined only by variables whose indices lie in $\bigcup_{j\in S}\operatorname{vbl}(A_j)$. Since the two collections of underlying random variables are disjoint and all $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ are mutually independent, these two collections generate independent events. Hence $A_i$ is independent of the family $\{A_j:j\in S\}$.

Since $S$ was arbitrary, the graph satisfies Definition 3.1. $\square$

### 3.2 Constraint-satisfaction interpretation

Let $q$ be a positive integer and define $[q]=\{1,\ldots,q\}$. Consider variables

$$
x_1,\ldots,x_n\in[q].
$$

A local constraint $C_i$ is specified by a set $S_i\subseteq[n]$ and a function

$$
C_i:[q]^{S_i}\to\{0,1\},
$$

where $C_i((x_r)_{r\in S_i})=1$ means that the constraint is satisfied. An assignment $(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\in[q]^n$ is a solution if every constraint is satisfied.

If the variables are sampled independently and uniformly from $[q]$, then each violated constraint defines a bad event

$$
A_i\coloneqq \{C_i((X_r)_{r\in S_i})=0\}.
$$

The graph connecting $i$ and $j$ whenever $S_i\cap S_j\neq\varnothing$ is then a dependency graph by Proposition 3.2.

## 4. The asymmetric Lovász Local Lemma

The asymmetric form is the fundamental statement. The symmetric form will follow as a corollary.

### Theorem 4.1 — Lovász Local Lemma, asymmetric form

Let $A_1,\ldots,A_m\in\mathcal F$ be bad events with dependency graph $G=([m],E)$. Suppose there exist real numbers

$$
\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_m\in[0,1)
$$

such that, for every $i\in[m]$,

$$
\Pr(A_i)
\le
\alpha_i
\prod_{j\in\Gamma(i)}(1-\alpha_j).
\tag{LLL}
$$

Then

$$
\Pr\!\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^m \overline{A_i}\right)
\ge
\prod_{i=1}^m (1-\alpha_i)
>0.
$$

Consequently, there exists at least one outcome $\omega\in\Omega$ for which none of $A_1,\ldots,A_m$ occurs.

### 4.1 Key conditional-probability lemma

For $S\subseteq[m]$, define

$$
B_S\coloneqq \bigcap_{j\in S}\overline{A_j}.
$$

Thus $B_S$ is the event that none of the bad events indexed by $S$ occurs.

### Lemma 4.2

Under the assumptions of Theorem 4.1, for every $S\subseteq[m]$ and every $i\in[m]\setminus S$,

$$
\Pr(B_S)>0
$$

and

$$
\Pr(A_i\mid B_S)\le \alpha_i.
\tag{1}
$$

#### Proof

We prove the two assertions simultaneously by induction on $|S|$.

#### Base case: $|S|=0$

Then $S=\varnothing$, so $B_S=\Omega$ and $\Pr(B_S)=1>0$. Moreover, by the LLL assumption,

$$
\Pr(A_i)
\le
\alpha_i\prod_{j\in\Gamma(i)}(1-\alpha_j)
\le \alpha_i,
$$

because every factor $1-\alpha_j$ lies in $(0,1]$. Thus (1) holds.

#### Induction step

Fix an integer $k\ge 1$, and assume the lemma holds for every set of size strictly less than $k$. Let $S\subseteq[m]$ satisfy $|S|=k$.

First we prove $\Pr(B_S)>0$. Choose any $r\in S$. Since $|S\setminus\{r\}|=k-1$, the induction hypothesis gives

$$
\Pr(B_{S\setminus\{r\}})>0
$$

and

$$
\Pr(A_r\mid B_{S\setminus\{r\}})\le \alpha_r<1.
$$

Therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Pr(B_S)
&=
\Pr(B_{S\setminus\{r\}})
\Pr(\overline{A_r}\mid B_{S\setminus\{r\}})\\
&=
\Pr(B_{S\setminus\{r\}})
\left(1-\Pr(A_r\mid B_{S\setminus\{r\}})\right)\\
&\ge
\Pr(B_{S\setminus\{r\}})(1-\alpha_r)\\
&>0.
\end{aligned}
$$

Now fix $i\notin S$. Partition $S$ into the neighbors and non-neighbors of $i$:

$$
N\coloneqq S\cap\Gamma(i),
\qquad
M\coloneqq S\setminus N.
$$

Thus every index in $M$ is a non-neighbor of $i$.

If $N=\varnothing$, then $S=M$ consists entirely of non-neighbors of $i$. By the definition of a dependency graph,

$$
\Pr(A_i\mid B_S)=\Pr(A_i)\le \alpha_i,
$$

so (1) holds.

Assume now that $N\neq\varnothing$. Since $B_S=B_N\cap B_M$,

$$
\Pr(A_i\mid B_S)
=
\frac{\Pr(A_i\cap B_N\mid B_M)}{\Pr(B_N\mid B_M)}.
\tag{2}
$$

The numerator satisfies

$$
\Pr(A_i\cap B_N\mid B_M)
\le
\Pr(A_i\mid B_M).
$$

All events indexed by $M$ are non-neighbors of $i$, so the dependency-graph property gives

$$
\Pr(A_i\mid B_M)=\Pr(A_i).
$$

Hence (2) implies

$$
\Pr(A_i\mid B_S)
\le
\frac{\Pr(A_i)}{\Pr(B_N\mid B_M)}.
\tag{3}
$$

Write

$$
N=\{j_1,\ldots,j_\ell\},
$$

where $\ell=|N|\ge 1$. By the chain rule,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Pr(B_N\mid B_M)
&=
\prod_{r=1}^{\ell}
\Pr\!\left(
\overline{A_{j_r}}
\,\middle|\,
B_M\cap\bigcap_{s=r+1}^{\ell}\overline{A_{j_s}}
\right)\\
&=
\prod_{r=1}^{\ell}
\left[
1-
\Pr\!\left(
A_{j_r}
\,\middle|\,
B_M\cap\bigcap_{s=r+1}^{\ell}\overline{A_{j_s}}
\right)
\right].
\end{aligned}
\tag{4}
$$

For each $r$, the conditioning event in (4) excludes

$$
|M|+(\ell-r)=k-r<k
$$

bad events. Therefore the induction hypothesis applies and yields

$$
\Pr\!\left(
A_{j_r}
\,\middle|\,
B_M\cap\bigcap_{s=r+1}^{\ell}\overline{A_{j_s}}
\right)
\le \alpha_{j_r}.
$$

Substituting this into (4),

$$
\Pr(B_N\mid B_M)
\ge
\prod_{r=1}^{\ell}(1-\alpha_{j_r})
=
\prod_{j\in N}(1-\alpha_j).
\tag{5}
$$

Since $N\subseteq\Gamma(i)$ and every factor $1-\alpha_j$ belongs to $(0,1]$,

$$
\prod_{j\in N}(1-\alpha_j)
\ge
\prod_{j\in\Gamma(i)}(1-\alpha_j).
\tag{6}
$$

Combining (3), (5), (6), and the LLL assumption,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Pr(A_i\mid B_S)
&\le
\frac{\Pr(A_i)}{\Pr(B_N\mid B_M)}\\
&\le
\frac{\alpha_i\prod_{j\in\Gamma(i)}(1-\alpha_j)}
{\prod_{j\in\Gamma(i)}(1-\alpha_j)}\\
&=\alpha_i.
\end{aligned}
$$

This completes the induction. $\square$

### 4.2 Proof of the asymmetric LLL

By the chain rule,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Pr\!\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^m\overline{A_i}\right)
&=
\prod_{i=1}^m
\Pr\!\left(
\overline{A_i}
\,\middle|\,
\bigcap_{j=1}^{i-1}\overline{A_j}
\right)\\
&=
\prod_{i=1}^m
\left[
1-
\Pr\!\left(
A_i
\,\middle|\,
\bigcap_{j=1}^{i-1}\overline{A_j}
\right)
\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

All conditioning events have positive probability by Lemma 4.2. Applying Lemma 4.2 once more,

$$
\Pr\!\left(
A_i
\,\middle|\,
\bigcap_{j=1}^{i-1}\overline{A_j}
\right)
\le \alpha_i.
$$

Therefore

$$
\Pr\!\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^m\overline{A_i}\right)
\ge
\prod_{i=1}^m(1-\alpha_i).
$$

Since every $\alpha_i<1$, each factor $1-\alpha_i$ is strictly positive. Hence

$$
\Pr\!\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^m\overline{A_i}\right)>0.
$$

This proves Theorem 4.1. $\square$

## 5. The symmetric Lovász Local Lemma

The asymmetric theorem is often applied in a situation where all bad-event probabilities admit the same upper bound and every event has at most the same number of dependencies.

Define

$$
p\coloneqq \max_{i\in[m]}\Pr(A_i),
\qquad
 d\coloneqq \max_{i\in[m]}|\Gamma(i)|.
$$

Let $e$ denote the base of the natural logarithm.

### Theorem 5.1 — Lovász Local Lemma, symmetric form

If

$$
ep(d+1)\le 1,
\tag{7}
$$

then

$$
\Pr\!\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^m\overline{A_i}\right)>0.
$$

#### Proof

We first assume $d\ge 1$. Set

$$
\alpha_1=\cdots=\alpha_m=\frac{1}{d+1}.
$$

For any $i\in[m]$, because $|\Gamma(i)|\le d$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\alpha_i\prod_{j\in\Gamma(i)}(1-\alpha_j)
&=
\frac{1}{d+1}
\left(1-\frac{1}{d+1}\right)^{|\Gamma(i)|}\\
&\ge
\frac{1}{d+1}
\left(\frac{d}{d+1}\right)^d.
\end{aligned}
\tag{8}
$$

The standard inequality

$$
\left(1+\frac1d\right)^d\le e
$$

implies

$$
\left(\frac{d}{d+1}\right)^d
=
\left(1+\frac1d\right)^{-d}
\ge e^{-1}.
$$

Thus (8) gives

$$
\alpha_i\prod_{j\in\Gamma(i)}(1-\alpha_j)
\ge
\frac{1}{e(d+1)}.
$$

Condition (7) is equivalent to

$$
p\le \frac{1}{e(d+1)}.
$$

Since $\Pr(A_i)\le p$, the asymmetric LLL condition holds for every $i$. Theorem 4.1 therefore gives the desired positive probability.

If $d=0$, the dependency graph has no edges, so every event is independent of all the others in the sense of Definition 3.1. Condition (7) gives $p\le 1/e<1$. Hence

$$
\Pr\!\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^m\overline{A_i}\right)
=
\prod_{i=1}^m(1-\Pr(A_i))>0.
$$

Thus the theorem holds for all $d\ge0$. $\square$

### 5.1 A slightly sharper symmetric sufficient condition

For $d\ge1$, the choice $\alpha_i=1/(d+1)$ actually shows that it is sufficient to have

$$
p
\le
\frac{1}{d+1}
\left(\frac{d}{d+1}\right)^d
=
\frac{d^d}{(d+1)^{d+1}}.
$$

The more familiar condition $ep(d+1)\le1$ is a convenient simplification of this bound.

### 5.2 A convenient $4pd$ criterion

The lecture also uses the following simpler sufficient condition.

### Corollary 5.2

Assume $d\ge1$. If

$$
4pd\le1,
\tag{9}
$$

then

$$
\Pr\!\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^m\overline{A_i}\right)>0.
$$

#### Proof

Set

$$
\alpha_1=\cdots=\alpha_m=\frac{1}{2d}.
$$

For every $i$,

$$
\alpha_i\prod_{j\in\Gamma(i)}(1-\alpha_j)
\ge
\frac{1}{2d}
\left(1-\frac{1}{2d}\right)^d.
$$

By Bernoulli's inequality,

$$
(1-x)^d\ge1-dx
\qquad\text{for }x\in[0,1].
$$

Taking $x=1/(2d)$,

$$
\left(1-\frac{1}{2d}\right)^d\ge\frac12.
$$

Therefore

$$
\alpha_i\prod_{j\in\Gamma(i)}(1-\alpha_j)
\ge\frac{1}{4d}.
$$

Condition (9) says $p\le1/(4d)$, so the asymmetric LLL condition holds. Apply Theorem 4.1. $\square$

## 6. Application: bounded-dependency $k$-SAT

Let $x_1,\ldots,x_n$ be Boolean variables taking values in $\{0,1\}$. A **literal** is either $x_r$ or its negation $\neg x_r$. A **clause** is a disjunction of literals. A **$k$-CNF formula** is a conjunction

$$
\Phi=C_1\wedge C_2\wedge\cdots\wedge C_m,
$$

where every clause $C_i$ contains exactly $k$ distinct variables.

Two clauses are said to **intersect** if they contain a common variable. Suppose every clause intersects at most $d$ other clauses.

### Theorem 6.1

If

$$
d\le 2^{k-2},
$$

then $\Phi$ is satisfiable.

#### Proof

Choose a uniformly random truth assignment by sampling $x_1,\ldots,x_n$ independently, each uniformly from $\{0,1\}$.

For each clause $C_i$, define the bad event

$$
A_i\coloneqq \{C_i\text{ is violated}\}.
$$

Because $C_i$ contains $k$ distinct variables, there is exactly one assignment to those $k$ variables that makes every literal in $C_i$ false. Therefore

$$
\Pr(A_i)=2^{-k}.
$$

Construct a dependency graph by joining $i$ and $j$ whenever $C_i$ and $C_j$ share a variable. If two clauses do not intersect, their bad events depend on disjoint sets of independent Boolean variables. By Proposition 3.2, this is a valid dependency graph. Its maximum degree is at most $d$.

Thus

$$
p=2^{-k}.
$$

The assumed inequality $d\le2^{k-2}$ is equivalent to

$$
4pd
=4\cdot 2^{-k}d
\le1.
$$

By Corollary 5.2,

$$
\Pr\!\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^m\overline{A_i}\right)>0.
$$

Hence there exists a truth assignment violating no clause. Such an assignment satisfies $\Phi$. $\square$

### 6.1 Comparison with the union bound

The union bound alone would require

$$
m2^{-k}<1,
$$

or equivalently $m<2^k$. The LLL replaces the global parameter $m$ by the local dependency degree $d$: the number of clauses may be very large, as long as each individual clause intersects only relatively few others.

## 7. Application: coloring a uniform hypergraph

A **hypergraph** is a pair

$$
H=(V,E),
$$

where $V$ is a finite vertex set and $E$ is a family of subsets of $V$, called **hyperedges**.

For a positive integer $k$, the hypergraph is **$k$-uniform** if every hyperedge has size exactly $k$; equivalently,

$$
E\subseteq \binom{V}{k}.
$$

For $v\in V$, define its degree by

$$
\deg_H(v)
\coloneqq
|\{e\in E:v\in e\}|.
$$

The maximum vertex degree is

$$
\Delta\coloneqq \max_{v\in V}\deg_H(v).
$$

Let $q$ be a positive integer. A **$q$-coloring** is a function

$$
f:V\to[q].
$$

For a hyperedge $e\in E$, define

$$
f(e)\coloneqq \{f(v):v\in e\}.
$$

The coloring is **proper** if no hyperedge is monochromatic, i.e.

$$
|f(e)|>1
\qquad\text{for every }e\in E.
$$

### Theorem 7.1

Let $H=(V,E)$ be a $k$-uniform hypergraph of maximum vertex degree $\Delta$. If

$$
\Delta\le \frac{q^{k-1}}{ek},
\tag{10}
$$

then $H$ admits a proper $q$-coloring.

#### Proof

If $E=\varnothing$, the conclusion is immediate. Assume $E\neq\varnothing$.

Color every vertex independently and uniformly with a random color in $[q]$. For every hyperedge $e\in E$, define the bad event

$$
A_e\coloneqq \{e\text{ is monochromatic}\}.
$$

There are $q$ possible monochromatic colors, and for any fixed color the probability that all $k$ vertices of $e$ receive that color is $q^{-k}$. Hence

$$
\Pr(A_e)=q\cdot q^{-k}=q^{1-k}.
$$

Thus we may take

$$
p=q^{1-k}.
$$

Two bad events $A_e$ and $A_{e'}$ depend on disjoint sets of vertex-color variables whenever $e\cap e'=\varnothing$. Therefore a valid dependency graph is obtained by connecting two distinct hyperedges exactly when they intersect.

Fix $e\in E$. Each vertex $v\in e$ belongs to at most $\Delta-1$ hyperedges other than $e$. Since $|e|=k$, the number of hyperedges distinct from $e$ that intersect $e$ is at most

$$
k(\Delta-1).
$$

Hence the dependency degree satisfies

$$
d\le k(\Delta-1).
$$

Since $k\ge1$,

$$
d+1
\le k(\Delta-1)+1
\le k\Delta.
$$

Using (10),

$$
\begin{aligned}
ep(d+1)
&\le
 e\,q^{1-k}\,k\Delta\\
&\le
 e\,q^{1-k}\,k\cdot\frac{q^{k-1}}{ek}\\
&=1.
\end{aligned}
$$

The symmetric LLL therefore gives

$$
\Pr\!\left(\bigcap_{e\in E}\overline{A_e}\right)>0.
$$

Thus, with positive probability, no hyperedge is monochromatic. Consequently a proper $q$-coloring exists. $\square$

## 8. How to apply the LLL

A typical application follows the same pattern.

1. **Choose a random experiment.** Define a probability distribution over candidate objects.
2. **Define bad events.** The desired object is obtained exactly when no bad event occurs.
3. **Construct a dependency graph.** Usually, two events are adjacent when they involve a common underlying random variable.
4. **Bound individual probabilities.** Find $p$ such that $\Pr(A_i)\le p$ for every $i$, or use event-specific bounds for the asymmetric form.
5. **Bound local dependency.** Find $d$ such that $|\Gamma(i)|\le d$, or retain the individual neighborhoods $\Gamma(i)$.
6. **Verify an LLL condition.** For example,
   $$
   ep(d+1)\le1,
   $$
   or, when $d\ge1$,
   $$
   4pd\le1.
   $$
   More generally, find $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_m\in[0,1)$ satisfying
   $$
   \Pr(A_i)\le
   \alpha_i\prod_{j\in\Gamma(i)}(1-\alpha_j)
   \qquad\text{for all }i.
   $$
7. **Conclude existence.** The LLL gives
   $$
   \Pr\!\left(\bigcap_i\overline{A_i}\right)>0,
   $$
   so at least one desired object exists.

## 9. Conceptual summary

The union bound uses only the marginal probabilities $\Pr(A_i)$ and treats every bad event as contributing globally. The Lovász Local Lemma additionally uses a dependency graph and exploits the fact that each event interacts with only a local neighborhood.

In the symmetric setting, the key quantities are

$$
p=\max_i\Pr(A_i)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
 d=\max_i|\Gamma(i)|.
$$

The standard condition

$$
ep(d+1)\le1
$$

formalizes the two requirements:

- each bad event is individually unlikely;
- each bad event depends on only a limited number of other bad events.

Under these conditions, the probability that **none** of the bad events occurs is strictly positive. The classical LLL is therefore an existence theorem: it proves that a desired configuration exists, without by itself providing an algorithm for finding one.

If $A_1,\ldots,A_m$ have a dependency graph and there exist $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_m\in[0,1)$ such that

$$
\Pr(A_i)
\le
\alpha_i\prod_{j\in\Gamma(i)}(1-\alpha_j)
\qquad\text{for every }i\in[m],
$$

then

$$
\Pr\!\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^m\overline{A_i}\right)
\ge
\prod_{i=1}^m(1-\alpha_i)>0.
$$

### Symmetric LLL

If

$$
p=\max_i\Pr(A_i),
\qquad
 d=\max_i|\Gamma(i)|,
$$

and

$$
ep(d+1)\le1,
$$

then

$$
\Pr\!\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^m\overline{A_i}\right)>0.

If

4pd\le1,
